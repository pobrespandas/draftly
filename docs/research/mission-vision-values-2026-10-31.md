# Mission, vision, and values: origins and use

Research date: 31 October 2026.

## Findings

Mission, vision, and values come from several traditions in organizational
management. The sources reviewed do not establish one inventor of the three-part
formula. Earlier statements of purpose and principles preceded the frameworks
that became common in strategic planning.

The three labels do not have one fixed meaning. In the Collins–Porras model,
vision includes purpose, values, and a desired future. Treating them as three
separate documents is a practical convention, not a requirement of that model.
See [Collins's explanation](https://www.jimcollins.com/article_topics/articles/aligning-action.html).

## Scope and method

The review asked where the concepts came from, how they became connected, and
what they can contribute to Draftly. It used author publications, university
records, an original corporate statement, and research summaries. A historical
editorial provides context for adoption; it does not establish first use.

This is a focused literature review, not a systematic review of all publications.
Dates below are publication or document dates, not search-engine crawl dates.
The report separates documented history from recommendations for Draftly.

## Historical development

### Statements of principles existed before the modern formula

Robert Wood Johnson wrote Johnson & Johnson's Credo in 1943. It describes
responsibilities to the people the company serves, employees, communities,
and shareholders. The company presents it as a guide to decisions.
This is an early documented example of corporate principles, not proof that
Johnson invented organizational values or the three-part formula.
[Source: J&J, Our Credo](https://www.jnj.com/our-credo).

### Drucker connected mission to purpose and action

Peter Drucker's account of mission asks what difference an organization exists
to make. The Drucker Institute's 2018 reading, adapted from his work, explains
mission through purpose rather than a list of methods. It also connects mission
to current decisions. This source establishes his approach; its 2018 date does
not establish when he first used the concept.
[Source: What Is Our Mission?](https://drucker.institute/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Reading_Drucker-on-Mission.pdf).

His self-assessment framework connects mission with customers, customer value,
results, and a plan. It is broader than writing a statement. The publisher's
description documents these five questions.
[Source: Wiley, The Five Most Important Questions](https://www.wiley.com/en-gb/the-five-most-important-questions-you-will-ever-ask-about-your-organization-p-9781118045404).

### Collins and Porras developed an explicit vision framework

Stanford records their *Organizational Vision and Visionary Organizations*
working paper as 1991, number 1159. The authors sought clearer concepts and
tools for setting organizational vision. This is a documented contribution,
not evidence that they invented the word or its organizational use.
[Source: Stanford working paper](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/working-papers/organizational-vision-visionary-organizations).

Their later worksheet separates core ideology from an envisioned future.
Core ideology includes purpose and values. The future includes a major goal
and a concrete description of achieving it. This structure differs from the
simple rule that mission means present and vision means future.
[Source: Collins, Vision Framework](https://www.jimcollins.com/tools/vision-framework.pdf).

### Combined approaches gained attention in the 1990s

Aurel Brudan's 2010 historical overview describes a growth in vision research
during the 1990s. It also describes growing interest late in that decade in
connecting mission, vision, values, and capabilities. This supports a gradual
development account. It does not identify a first author of the exact triad.
[Source: Performance Magazine](https://www.performancemagazine.org/vision-statements-as-strategic-management-tools-%E2%80%93-historical-overview/).

## What the labels can mean

The following distinctions are useful for Draftly. They are working definitions,
not universal historical definitions.

| Concept | Question | Useful content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mission | Why does the product exist, and whom does it help? | Its purpose and intended contribution. |
| Vision | What future do we want to help create? | A direction that can guide choices. |
| Values | What matters when choices conflict? | Principles expressed through decisions. |
| Strategy | What will we choose to do? | Priorities, trade-offs, and resource choices. |
| Goals | What result will we check? | Outcomes with an observable completion condition. |

Collins explicitly groups mission or purpose, core values, and future ambitions
under vision. His article also emphasizes alignment between values and actual
practices. A document alone is not that alignment.
[Source: Aligning Action and Values](https://www.jimcollins.com/article_topics/articles/aligning-action.html).

## Evidence of usefulness and its limits

Desmidt, Prinzie, and Decramer's 2011 meta-analysis reports a small positive
relationship between mission statements and financial performance. Measurement
differences affected the estimates. The accessible publisher abstract supports
that limited finding; this review did not inspect the complete study dataset.
[Source: Management Decision, DOI 10.1108/00251741111120806](https://doi.org/10.1108/00251741111120806).

That association does not prove that creating these Markdown files will improve
Draftly. It also does not test the exact combination of mission, vision, values,
and a software constitution. Using the documents as decision aids is a product
choice, not a demonstrated causal result of the cited research.

## Application to Draftly

The existing documents separate purpose, direction, values, and decision
principles:

- [MISSION.md](../../MISSION.md) describes support through the writing process.
- [VISION.md](../../VISION.md) describes a trusted space for ideas and workflows.
- [VALUES.md](../../VALUES.md) lists writing, flexibility, trust, clarity,
  autonomy, and simplicity.
- [CONSTITUTION.md](../../CONSTITUTION.md) applies principles to product,
  design, and engineering choices.
- [PRODUCT.md](../../PRODUCT.md) records audience and priorities.

Recommendation: keep this separation if it makes decisions easier. Review the
documents against concrete choices. For example, trust can guide recovery and
backup behavior; clarity can guide claims on the website; simplicity can guide
the first capture flow. These examples are our interpretation, not findings
from the historical sources.

The constitution is Draftly's chosen decision guide. This research does not
establish it as a standard fourth part of the management framework. No product
statement, glossary definition, or architecture decision was changed here.

## Open questions

- The first published use of the exact three-part formula remains unverified.
- Earlier references to Barnard, Drucker, and Selznick need original-edition
  checks before assigning first-use dates or direct quotations.
- The consulted history concerns organizational management, not the complete
  linguistic or religious history of the words.
- Draftly still needs concrete experience to judge whether each document helps
  decisions or repeats another document.

All linked external sources were consulted on 31 October 2026. The J&J Credo,
Drucker reading, Collins article, and Collins worksheet were read directly.
The Stanford record and publisher summaries establish publication details and
reported claims. Full-text access to some publisher pages was restricted.
